WRITE UP:

Introduction:

The dataset used is the Chicago Crimes dataset. Each item in the data is either a reported incident or a record for each victim of a murder. Crime Type, Arrest Status, and Year were selected to provide more detail about each crime. Each of these variables can be filtered on in the visualizations above. Latitude, Longitude, Community Area, and Police District were used as geographical variables. Latitude and Longitude are used to plot observations on the map, and the Community Area is listed in tooltips as the GeoJSON used breaks Chicago down by community areas. In the bar chart, arrests are broken down by Police District in order to investigate differing arrest trends and behaviors across districts, each linked to a districts arrest trends overtime on a line graph.

Rationale:

Category 1:
Dynamic filters and queries were implemented with dropdowns and sliders for the variables listed above.
Brushing and Linking was implemented between visualizations. Points brushed on the map will display their cumulative arrests by district on the bar graph. Since linked data are only displayed on the bar graph, the decision to revert to the bar graph when brushing was an intentional design choice.
Category 2:
Panning and zooming was implemented on the map.
Tooltips were implemented for each mark on the map, as well as for bars on the bar chart and marks on the line plot.


Given the nature of data visualizations, we wanted to show meaningful data trends, and through the bar chart and the line graph we were able to do so. With these visualizations we can see where, how, and when crime has occurred allowing viewers to understand which areas may need more police attention, may be safer, and be trending upward/downward in terms of safety in their community. When starting to create these it was immediately clear that the data had to be aggregated and use a smaller number of variables to truly tell the story of crime and areas in need of more police attention. After data aggregation focus was shifted to building an interactive bar chart from this data.

When thinking of dynamic queries and filters, having a bar graph was an easy decision to make. A bar graph is simple allowing immediate insights to be gained from the data, portray each independent district in a uniform categorical manner, and allow numerous follow-up questions to be asked. These follow up questions are where the queries come in which are much more favorable to implement on a bar chart. The filter/query options implemented here were: being able to filter between the minimum arrests made, crime type, arrests per district, and year range of arrest data.

It was decided to count arrests by district rather than community area as it was believed there would be a greater relationship between police district and community area; since arrests are made by police, it stood to reason that the geographical categorization more closely tied to the police would better reveal patterns in the data. Focusing in on the query of how many arrests were there per district per year, it became clear that a whole new data set and graph needed to be implemented, specifically a line graph accessible by click linked to each district to portray the trend of arrests made in each district over the years. While seeing all the arrests together tells one story, seeing the actual trend of arrests over time can show even more. The combo of the bar graph along with the line graph ended up being a very effective combo to showcase the true arrest trend in each district.

While everything visualization-wise seemed to work, it turned out that the aggregated y-axis was having issues showing accurate arrest data. Some districts had extremely high arrest counts (~100,000) while others had very small arrest counts (~3) usually making the smaller one practically invisible. This was troublesome at first, but after looking back over class slides it became clear that a logarithmic scale would solve this issue, and thus the problem was solved and the graphs were ready to be merged with this page. A symlog scale was used in actual practice, so as to allow arrest counts of 1 to display properly.

For interaction with the map, panning and zooming were obvious choices.
Due to there being too many different values for district, community area, and crime type, tooltips were chosen to communicate these values rather than trying to find different colors or shapes for each unique value of these attributes.
A map was chosen in the first place as it is one of the quickest and most effective ways to illustrate the geographical distribution of the data. From there, the above interactions emerged naturally.
Fill was used to encode arrest status on the map, as it seemed more effective than shape. Shape may have required more focus to discern and overlapping points may have interfered with identifying the shape of a given mark. Since arrest is a boolean value, fill works well for this attribute.
Color was used to denote points selected by brushing. Since the point of brushing was to link to data on the other visualization that only encoded arrests, only points that have true as an arrest value are highlighted.
